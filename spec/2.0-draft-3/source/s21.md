# 21. Security and Privacy Considerations

Δ NEW (v2-draft-3) --- An analysis of what HCP protects against and what it leaves to others (C28, D4; SP1--SP12). Its place, as §21 in its own Part, follows RS2.

This section is informative. It explains what each part of HCP protects against, what remains, and who acts on what remains. Its requirements are stated in the sections it cites, not here. It is organized by HCP's building blocks --- entries, grants, disclosure, rules, audit, and discovery --- and then by topics that cut across them.

## 21.1 What HCP Does Not Prevent

Δ NEW (v2-draft-3) --- The non-goal, first (SP3, D4).

HCP limits what context reaches a client, records what it did, and lets the person see and undo it. It does not govern what happens beyond that.

- **A model's other capabilities.** HCP limits what context a model receives and what it may write. A general-purpose model with tools can act destructively whether or not its client conforms to HCP.
- **What a client does with context it received.** The provider cannot observe use after disclosure. Purposes (6.1.5.2), use-until (7.6), onward disclosure (3.12), and directives (6.4) are obligations a client takes on; HCP records them, and accountability for keeping them lies with the client and its operator.
- **Whether a client tells the truth about itself.** Its purposes, its claim to follow directives (18.1), and its declared legal retention (6.2.5) are declarations. HCP makes each one visible to the person and records it; it cannot verify it.

HCP's protections are therefore of two kinds: limits that the provider enforces, and records that make a broken obligation visible to the person and to anyone holding a client to account.

## 21.2 Entries

Δ NEW (v2-draft-3) --- Threats to entries (SP2, SP12).

- **False attribution.** A client can claim that the person said something they did not. Such an entry is recorded as `unverified` until the person confirms it, and has no standing in a conflict (3.7, 4.7.1).
- **Inferences presented as facts.** A model's guess is labelled `model-inferred`, carries the asserter's own confidence and, ideally, its basis (4.2.5). It cannot outrank the person or an attestation (4.7). In health it is withheld until confirmed (16.2).
- **Institutional records.** An attestation is never quarantined or deprecated by a conflict or by the person's acceptance of another entry (4.4, 4.7.3). The person's correction therefore cannot silently rewrite it, and it cannot silently override the person.
- **Proposals that steer.** A proposal for resolving a conflict comes with its author's reasons and changes nothing until the person acts (4.7.4). A provider or client that proposes the same outcome every time can still steer a person who accepts by habit. The audit log records who proposed what, and pilots report how often proposals were accepted (G1.2).
- **What a basis reveals.** `assertion.basis` is free text written by a client, and it is disclosed with the entry. The rule that it identifies no one but the person cannot be checked by the provider (4.2.5).
- **Export integrity.** An export is not yet signed. A receiving provider cannot tell whether it was altered on the way (P1.4).

## 21.3 Grants

Δ NEW (v2-draft-3) --- Threats to grants, including sign-on strength (SP2, SP6).

- **Misleading consent screens.** A client could describe its request in flattering terms. Categories are described to the person as the provider describes them, not as the client does (4.5), and purposes are shown before approval (6.1.6). How screens are designed to avoid consent fatigue is guidance (G4).
- **How strongly the person was identified.** A grant is only as strong as the sign-in behind it. A sign-in through a messaging application rests on a phone number, which can be moved to another SIM card. The strength of the authentication behind each grant is recorded in `grant.issued` as `acr`, and a grant reaching a High-sensitivity category is expected to rest on more than a messaging sign-in alone (6.1.6). Deployments with low connectivity may decide otherwise, and give up that assurance.
- **Stolen tokens.** Access tokens are short-lived, audience-restricted, and checked against the grant's status on every request (13.4, 13.6). Refresh tokens for public clients are sender-constrained or rotated.
- **Delegates.** A delegate's authority is bounded by scope, term, `basis.covers`, and standing rules (17.4). A sub-delegate's is bounded by its delegate's, and reaches one level only (17.10). The person can revoke every grant either authorizes (17.5).
- **Revocation and copies.** Revocation ends access and makes disclosed copies unusable once the client is notified (6.2.2). It cannot recall a copy a client keeps unlawfully, and does not reach a copy kept under a declared legal obligation (6.2.5).

## 21.4 Disclosure

Δ NEW (v2-draft-3) --- Threats to disclosure, and trust in receiving systems (SP2, SP9). Onward sharing (OS8).

- **Sweeping a category.** A client that asks many rephrased queries can gather more than any one query returns. The quantity bound limits each response, and a provider may rate-limit a pattern of rephrased queries (7.2).
- **Linking a person across clients.** A disclosure carries a grant-scoped reference rather than the entry's identifier, and withholds the provenance that names other clients (7.4). Two clients comparing notes can still match content itself. Provenance on request reveals the names of contributing clients only to the person's own assistant (7.4.1).
- **Copies outliving their use.** Use-until is an obligation, not a technical control (7.6). A client without a notification endpoint receives short-lived copies.
- **Onward sharing.** Where the person allowed it, a client may tell other organizations what a task needs from Low-sensitivity context (3.12). The receiving organization is outside HCP: no grant, use-until or revocation reaches it. The protections are the limits on what may pass (Low sensitivity only, the client's own words, no entries or credentials), the person's separate election (6.1.4), standing rules and directives, and the report of each share in the person's log (5.2.8). A client that shares without reporting cannot be detected by the provider, but has broken a rule it took on (21.1).
- **Receiving systems.** Before approval, a person can know a client's operator, its purposes, its claims, and any legal retention (6.1.6). They cannot yet require evidence of how the receiving system protects what it receives. Recipient assurance --- attested environments, declared protection regimes, trust registries --- is later work (P1.4).

## 21.5 Rules

Δ NEW (v2-draft-3) --- Standing rules and directives (SP2, SP12).

- **Standing rules** are enforced by the provider, and apply across every grant from the moment they are set (6.3).
- **Directives** bind only a client that claims to follow them, and only as far as it keeps its claim (6.4). The provider cannot observe compliance. Directives are delivered with every disclosure, so a client cannot say it did not know them.
- **What a directive's text reveals.** A directive's wording reaches every client it applies to. "Never share my HIV status" discloses what it means to protect. The Management Client is expected to warn the person, and to suggest scoping such a directive to its category (6.4).
- **Unknown actions.** A client that does not recognize a directive's action is not to treat it as a permission (6.4). It can still misread the person's words.

## 21.6 Audit

Δ NEW (v2-draft-3) --- Tampering and the controller-side log (SP2, SP7).

- **Tampering.** Append-only means the log cannot be altered through HCP; it does not detect alteration made outside it. Providers declare the log's integrity (8.7). A provider holding High-sensitivity categories is expected to declare at least `hash-chained`. A common format for chains and checkpoints is later work (P1.4).
- **Joining the two logs.** The controller-side log may outlive a person's data, and is designed so that it cannot be joined to the person's own log to reconstruct content or infer it from access patterns (8.5). A pseudonym stable across the operator's whole population would allow that join; a per-person or rotating pseudonym resists it.
- **Who reads the log.** Clients never do (8.3). A delegate reads the events of grants they or their sub-delegates authorized, and a former delegate keeps a limited view, because consenting for someone else carries accountability (17.7).
- **Volume.** Read logging is costly, and compaction waits for evidence of what it costs (P1.4).

## 21.7 Discovery and Identity

Δ NEW (v2-draft-3) --- How clients, issuers and wallets are identified (SP4, SP5).

- **Who a client is.** A client is identified by its registration, which names its operator (3.2.8). A client that writes `issuer-attested` entries, or claims to follow directives, is expected to authenticate with an asymmetric key rather than a shared secret (13.6). Identifying a client by a metadata document at an HTTPS URL, as MCP and other agent protocols do, lets a provider see who a client is without prior registration. It does not tell the provider whether the client is trustworthy.
- **Who an issuer is.** An issuer's key is found from its identifier, by DID resolution where it is a DID (14.6). An issuer authorizes a client to attest for it by a credential the provider can verify (14.10). A recorded arrangement is recorded as `unverified`, because the provider has only its own word for it (3.7).
- **Wallets.** A wallet that holds a person's credentials or keys is part of their trust domain where it acts only for them (4.8). How a wallet proves what it is to a provider is not specified.
- **Discovery documents.** A discovery document is served over HTTPS from the provider's base URL. A client trusts it as far as it trusts that origin (19).

## 21.8 Keys

Δ NEW (v2-draft-3) --- Key custody (SP8).

| Key | Held by | What its compromise allows |
|----------------------------|--------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Revocation notification signing key | HCP Provider (9.9) | Forged revocation notices, which make clients discard copies they could lawfully use. A forged notice cannot grant access. |
| Issuer signing key | Issuer | Forged credentials that verify, until the issuer revokes the key or its credentials (14.7) |
| Attestation authorization | Client, issued by the issuer (14.10) | Writes attributed to the issuer, within the authorization's scope, until it is revoked |
| Content keys under `e2e` | The Context Subject's trust domain (4.8) | Reading the person's content. Losing every copy loses the content (G1.4). |
| Client credentials | Client | Acting as the client within its grants, until revoked (6.2) |

Rotation, storage in hardware where available, and recovery that does not hand keys to an operator are deployment matters. The table says what each key protects, so that a deployment knows what is at stake.

## 21.9 The Trust Domain

Δ NEW (v2-draft-3) --- Threats under end-to-end encrypted storage (SP12).

Under end-to-end encrypted storage, the component that holds the person's keys does the work that reads content (4.8). It becomes a target in its own right.

- **Compromise.** Whoever controls the component can read the person's content and answer queries as it chooses. It runs on a device or service the person controls, so its security is theirs to keep, with whatever help the deployment gives.
- **Vouching for a forgery.** The component verifies credentials for the provider, and the result is recorded with `verification.by` `trust-domain` (4.2.2). Where a person stands to gain from a forged credential --- an age threshold, eligibility --- a component under their control could vouch for one. A client making a consequential decision on a credential is expected to verify it itself, under either storage posture (3.6).
- **Unavailability.** A query that needs an unreachable component fails with subject unavailable (5.5). An attacker who can keep the component offline can deny service, but cannot obtain weaker answers.
- **What remains visible.** The operator still sees categories, timing, and provenance (4.8). For some people, holding a category at all is sensitive.

## 21.10 Models

Δ NEW (v2-draft-3) --- Context as untrusted input to models (SP10).

- **Context as untrusted input.** An entry's content was written by some client, and may carry text crafted to steer a model that later reads it: an instruction hidden in a note, a "directive" that is not one. A client is expected to present disclosed content, and the text of directives, to a model as data and never as instructions (7.4).
- **Directives and models.** Directives bind the client, not the model. Under the MCP Binding the host applies them; placing a directive in a model's context is not following it (10.5).
- **What models write.** A model's inferences are labelled as such and carry no standing against the person (4.4, 4.7). A model that writes repeatedly can still crowd a category with guesses; where inferences from the same asserter contradict each other and neither is confirmed, the later one prevails (4.7).
- **Intermediaries.** A model or service between the person and a client may owe the person no duty at all. Loyalty obligations for such intermediaries are the subject of fiduciary governance work outside HCP (I10).

## 21.11 Which Values the Defaults Put First

Δ NEW (v2-draft-3) --- The values behind the defaults, and their costs (SP11).

Every default in HCP favours some values over others. Learning Economy Foundation reviewers, raising a point from Carissa, asked that this be said plainly.

HCP's defaults put the person's autonomy and privacy first:

- No client receives context without a grant the person approved, for purposes they saw (6.1.6).
- Copies are short-lived by default, and shorter for sensitive categories (7.6).
- Standing rules only deny, and directives only narrow (6.3, 6.4).
- Inferences wait for the person in health, and never outrank them (16.2, 4.7).
- Conflicts are settled by the person, not by the provider (4.7.3).

These choices have costs, and the costs fall on others as well as on the person:

- **Care coordination.** A clinic, a caregiver, or a teacher may not see context that would help, because the person never granted it, or granted it briefly.
- **Emergencies.** HCP has no emergency access. A clinician cannot reach context the person has not granted, however urgent the need. Emergency access is later work (P1.4).
- **The work of consent.** Every grant is a decision the person makes. People asked too often stop reading what they approve (G4).
- **Relationships.** Rules that only deny cannot express "share this with my family". A delegate or a grant does that work, more slowly.

A deployment may tune some of these within the specification: use-until within its floor and ceilings, how consent is presented (G4), and how delegations are set up (17). It may not tune away the grant, the audit log, or the person's power to revoke.
